### <mark>Abstract</mark> 

Objectives: To investigate the integration of multimodal AI-generated reports into radiology workflow over time, focusing on their impact on efficiency, acceptability, and report quality. 

Methods: A multicase, multireader study involved 756 publicly available chest radiographs interpreted by five radiologists using preliminary reports generated by a radiology-specific multimodal AI model, divided into 7 sequential batches of 108 radiographs each. Two thoracic radiologists assessed the final reports using RADPEER criteria for agreement and 5-point Likert scale for quality. Reading times, rate of acceptance without modification, agreement, and quality scores were measured, with statistical analyses evaluating trends across seven sequential batches. 

Results: Radiologists’ reading times for chest radiographs decreased from 25.8 seconds in batch 1 to 19.3 seconds in batch 7 ( _P <_ .001). Acceptability increased from 54.6% to 60.2% ( _P <_ .001), with normal chest radiographs demonstrating high rates (68.9%) compared with abnormal chest radiographs (52.6%; _P <_ .001). Median agreement and quality scores remained stable for normal chest radiographs but varied significantly for abnormal chest radiographs (all _P <_ .05). 

Discussion: The introduction of AI-generated reports improved efficiency of chest radiograph interpretation, and acceptability increased over time. However, agreement and quality scores showed variability, particularly in abnormal cases, emphasizing the need for oversight in the interpretation of complex chest radiographs. 

Key Words: Chest radiographs, generative AI, radiologist-AI interaction, report generation

## METHODS 

This retrospective study using public dataset was exempted from review by the local institutional review board. The study used 756 frontal chest radiographs from the publicly available PadChest dataset, randomly selected via Python (3.11.5) [8]. Out of these, 223 (29.5%) were labeled as normal (without any abnormality), and 533 (70.5%) were labeled as abnormal (with abnormalities) based on annotations provided by the original publisher (Table 1). The study used a radiology-specific multimodal generative AI model for chest radiograph report generation (KARA-CXR, Kakaobrain, Seoul, South Korea, for research use only). The 

model processes chest radiographs as input and generates corresponding radiologic reports as output. It identifies abnormalities using an encoder-classifier system and produces reports via a transformer-based captioner, designed specifically to produce “findings-only” radiology reports, without additional impressions or recommendations [4]. 

Five radiologists, with 7 to 15 years of experience, including three specializing in chest, one in breast, and one in general radiology, independently interpreted the chest radiographs by reviewing and directly editing the AI-generated preliminary reports to create the final version, while being blinded to clinical and label information [9]. Chest radiograph interpretation was conducted using a custombuilt reporting platform. Before the study, each radiologist reviewed 10 sample chest radiographs (not included in the study cohort) to become familiar with the reporting environment. The 756 study radiographs were divided into 7 sequential batches of 108 cases and interpreted over a 1- month period in November 2023; no fixed time interval was enforced between batches. Case order was randomized, and reading times were recorded automatically by the system. 

Thereafter, two experienced thoracic radiologists with over 20 years of experience, blinded to clinical information and reader experience, assessed the agreement and quality of the final reports. Agreement scores were rated using RADPEER scoring system as follows: (1) clinically significant discrepancy; (2) clinically nonsignificant discrepancy; (3) clinically significant understandable miss; (4) clinically nonsignificant and understandable miss, which is justifiable given the complexity, subtlety, or difficulty of the case; and (5) complete agreement [10]. Quality scores of the final reports were assessed subjectively on a 5-point Likert scale: (1) very poor; (2) poor; (3) average; (4) good; and (5) excellent. Figure 1 illustrates the study design and workflow, and Figure 2 demonstrates examples of chest radiographs and the corresponding AI-generated reports. 

For each batch, average reading times and the acceptance rate of AI-generated reports were analyzed. Acceptability was defined as a case in which no revisions were made to the AI-generated report before it was approved by the radiologist. In addition, we assessed the proportion of cases with complete agreement, median agreement, and quality scores. Complete agreement was defined as cases when two experienced thoracic radiologists, in consensus, rated the final report as 5, complete agreement. 

Statistical comparisons were made between radiographs with and without abnormal findings, as well as between thoracic and nonthoracic radiologists. Variables that demonstrated normal distribution were analyzed using _t_ tests. Nonnormally distributed and ordinal data (eg, agreement and quality scores) were assessed using the Mann-Whitney _U_ test for the comparison between two groups. Additionally,

## RESULTS 

Reading times for chest radiographs significantly decreased over batches, from 25.8 ± 29.7 seconds in batch 1 to 19.3 ± 29.0 seconds in batch 7 ( _P <_ .001). Reading times for normal radiographs were consistently shorter than those for abnormal radiographs in all batches ( _P <_ .001) (Fig. 3a). 

Acceptability rates of AI-generated reports increased over batches, from 54.6% (295 of 540) in batch 1 to 60.2% (325 of 540) in batch 7 ( _P <_ .001) (Fig. 3b). Normal radiographs had higher acceptability rates (68.9% [768 of 1,115]) than abnormal radiographs (52.6% [1,403 of 2,665]) ( _P <_ .001). Interestingly, thoracic radiologists exhibited higher acceptance rates for AI-generated reports 

(76.7% [1,739 of 2,268]) compared with nonthoracic radiologists (28.6% [432 of 1,512]) ( _P <_ .001). 

Complete agreement rates of final reports rated by two experienced thoracic radiologists did not change significantly across batches, 52.2% (282 of 540) in batch 1 to 63.2% (343 of 540) in batch 7 ( _P_ = .087) (Fig. 3c). Complete agreement was achieved in 56.6% (2,139 of 3,780) of all cases, with normal radiographs showing a higher agreement rate (57.7% [643 of 1,115]) compared with abnormal radiographs (56.1% [1,496 of 2,665]) ( _P <_ .001). 

Agreement scores of the final reports had a consistent median of 5.0 (interquartile range [IQR] 4.5-5.0) across all batches and groups, showing no statistically significant change across batches for normal radiographs ( _P_ =.570). In contrast, abnormal radiographs exhibited statistically significant differences across batches ( _P_ = .003). Quality scores maintained a median of 4.5 (IQR 4.5-5.0) across batches, with normal radiographs generally receiving higher scores than abnormal radiographs ( _P <_ .001). Additionally, quality scores demonstrated statistically significant differences across batches for both normal and abnormal radiographs ( _P <_ .001 for both).

## DISCUSSION 

The results of this study demonstrate that AI-generated reports were associated with a reduction in chest radiograph interpretation times over the study period, with reading times decreasing over time from 25.8 ± 29.7 seconds in the first batch to 19.3 ± 29.0 seconds in the last batch ( _P <_ .001). Acceptability of AI-generated reports without modification increased from 54.6% in the first batch to 60.2% in the last batch ( _P <_ .001), suggesting growing confidence in AIgenerated reports over time. Radiographs without abnormal findings had higher acceptability (68.9%) than those with abnormal findings (52.6%) ( _P <_ .001), reflecting the requirement of human oversight and revision in radiographs with abnormal findings. However, the proportion of complete agreement, rated by experienced thoracic radiologists, showed no significant change across batches ( _P_ = .087). Agreement scores for normal radiographs did not change over time ( _P_ = .570), and abnormal radiographs showed 

variability across batches without discernable trend ( _P_ = .003). Similarly, quality scores for normal radiographs were consistent, and those for abnormal radiographs demonstrated significant variability without a clear trend ( _P <_ .001). Although the methodology is cited from our earlier publication [9], this study introduces a novel longitudinal analysis that examines radiologist-AI interaction over sequential batches, characterizing temporal variation in efficiency, acceptability, and report quality. 

The current study provides an initial exploration of radiologist-AI interaction in radiology by employing multimodal generative AI for creating preliminary radiology reports. Unlike prior research that primarily addressed binary classifications or abnormality detection, this study investigates how AI-generated outputs may fit into radiologist workflows as preliminary reports [6]. It also provides brief insights into how radiologists engage with AI-generated reports, noting that although reading times decreased and acceptability increased over time, the quality of reports showed no significant difference in normal radiographs and changed without improvement in abnormal radiographs. These findings suggest that future studies should explore ways to enhance the accuracy of reports when using preliminary AIgenerated reports and examine how increasing confidence in AI impacts radiologists’ decision making and diagnostic performance. 

Despite the increasing acceptability of AI-generated reports over time, overall diagnostic concordance of the final reports remained unchanged. This suggests that although radiologists grew more comfortable approving AI-generated reports without modifications, this did not necessarily impact the diagnostic accuracy of the final reports. Moreover, although reductions in interpretation time and increases in acceptability were statistically significant, it may not yet reflect clinically meaningful improvements in workflow or diagnostic performance. Future studies, ideally conducted prospectively in realworld clinical settings, are needed to determine whether sustained exposure to AI-generated reports alters radiologists’ acceptance thresholds and how such changes may affect clinical decision making and patient outcomes. 

The current study has limitations. First, including the small number of participating radiologists, which may limit the ability to assess differences across radiologists with various subspecialty training and years of experience. Second, the variability in the distribution of abnormal radiographs across batches could have influenced the results. Third, the study did not incorporate qualitative feedback from participating radiologists, and future work may benefit from interviews or surveys to better capture their perceptions and experiences when interacting with AI-generated preliminary reports. Lastly, radiologists were blinded to clinical information to control for potential confounders, however, this does not reflect real-world practice and may limit the generalizability of the findings to routine clinical settings. Future research involving a larger cohort of readers in a prospective setting is necessary to identify trends in radiologists’ interaction with AI systems and to distinguish beneficial experiential adaptations, such as improved efficiency and decision making, from potential risks associated with overreliance on AI-generated outputs. Developing structured evaluation frameworks that assess and mitigate these interactions could provide valuable insights into optimizing the integration of AI in clinical workflows while maintaining diagnostic accuracy and safety. 

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of multimodal generative AI in improving efficiency in 

radiology workflows. However, further research is needed to evaluate its impact on report quality and radiologist decision making.

- n Acceptability, defined as the proportion of AIgenerated reports approved without requiring revisions, increased from 54.6% in batch 1 to 60.2% in batch 7 ( _P <_ .001). Acceptability was higher for normal radiographs (68.9%) than for abnormal radiographs (52.6%; _P <_ .001). 

- n Agreement scores (RADPEER criteria) and quality scores (5-point Likert scale) for normal radiographs remained stable at a median of 5.0 (IQR 4.5-5.0) and 4.5 (IQR 4.5-5.0), respectively. In contrast, abnormal radiographs displayed significant variability without a clear trend across batches in both agreement ( _P_ = .003) and quality scores ( _P <_ .001).
